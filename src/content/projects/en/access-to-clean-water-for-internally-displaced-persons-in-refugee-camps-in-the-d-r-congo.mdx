## The project

### The situation on the ground

More than 45,000 Burundian refugees live in the Lusenda and Mulongwe refugee camps in the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, spread across 67 villages. They fled their home country because of political crises and violence and now live in conditions where basic infrastructure is inadequate or overstretched.

Access to clean drinking water remains a key challenge: cholera, typhoid and diarrhoeal diseases are widespread and pose a particular threat to children, pregnant women and older people. The population depends on contaminated water from streams and unprotected springs.

## Our local partner: APM

**À Portée de Mains (APM)** is a locally rooted aid organisation in the Democratic Republic of the Congo that has been supporting Burundian refugees in the Lusenda and Mulongwe camps in South Kivu province for several years. APM works in the fields of WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene), education, women’s empowerment and child protection, with a particular focus on participatory project work.

APM is led by **Sango Shila**, an activist from the region who knows the structural challenges on the ground first-hand. With a solution-oriented and transparent approach, he has built APM into a reliable partner for international NGOs and funding institutions.

<Figure src="wp/2025/12/APM_51.jpg" alt="Access to Clean Water for Internally Displaced Persons in Refugee Camps in the D.R. Congo (photo 2)" />

The official government body responsible for managing refugee camps in the DR Congo, the **Commission Nationale de Réfugiés (CNR)**, has explicitly recognised the joint work of APM and WeWater and now counts WeWater among the **key actors in the WASH sector** in the camps – at a time when many other WASH actors have left the region following the withdrawal of major international donors.

---

### Phase 1: Getting started with six filters

In the first project phase, WeWater and APM installed **6 AQQAcube filters** in the two camps – three in Lusenda and three in Mulongwe.

#### Preparation and site selection

To prepare, two strategy meetings were held with all relevant local stakeholders: the CNR, the camp management committees, community leaders, refugee representatives and APM’s technical team. WeWater was kept informed and involved throughout.

The meetings focused in particular on:

- the filter locations
- roles and responsibilities for operation and maintenance
- logistics and transport
- involving and training the local community

#### Transporting the filters

The water filters were shipped from Germany and imported via **Melchior Ndadaye International Airport** in **Bujumbura (Burundi)**. From there they travelled to **Uvira**, then – because the roads were impassable – by motorised pirogue across Lake Tanganyika to **Baraka**, and finally by four-wheel-drive vehicle to the camps. Despite the complex route, the transport went according to plan and safely.

<Figure src="wp/2026/05/WhatsApp-Image-2026-04-07-at-14.54.50.jpg" alt="Access to Clean Water for Internally Displaced Persons in Refugee Camps in the D.R. Congo (photo 3)" />

#### Awareness-raising and training

Before installation, information sessions were held in the camps. Staff from APM, the CNR and the local WASH committee explained the filters’ **function, maintenance and lifespan** – in **Kirundi** and **Swahili** to ensure the best possible understanding. Specific target groups took part in practical demonstrations to learn how to handle the filters safely.

#### Installation

Three water filters were installed in each camp:

- **Lusenda:** at the main health centre, at an additional health post and at the camp committee office
- **Mulongwe:** in the densely populated Block 14, at the health centre and at the administration office

To run the filters, management committees of five members each were set up in both camps – at least **80% of them women**. They are responsible for the use and maintenance of the filters, regular awareness sessions and fair access to the filtered water. The committees received training in community governance and continue to receive technical support.

<Figure src="wp/2025/12/APM_136.jpg" alt="Access to Clean Water for Internally Displaced Persons in Refugee Camps in the D.R. Congo (photo 4)" />

#### First impact

The six installed filters already provided around **7,500 households** with clean drinking water. Initial feedback from the health centres confirmed a **significant reduction in waterborne diseases** in the areas served. You can find the report including the data collected here: **[50% fewer illnesses thanks to WeWater filters in refugee camps in the DR Congo](/blog/50-prozent-weniger-krankheiten/).** However, the filters are not enough for the number of people living there, which is why we and our partners decided to install more filters in a second phase.

---

### Phase 2: Six more filters – greater reach, new strategy

The first phase clearly showed that the AQQAcube technology is accepted, works reliably and noticeably reduces illness. But six filters are nowhere near enough to supply more than 45,000 residents. APM and WeWater therefore jointly procured and installed **6 more AQQAcube filters** – again three in Lusenda and three in Mulongwe. This means a total of **12 filters** are now in use across both camps.

#### Site selection based on lessons from Phase 1

In consultation with the CNR, the camp management, community representatives and the WASH committee, the most densely populated areas of the camps were identified and specifically selected for the new installations:

- **Lusenda:** areas Lusenda 1, Lusenda 2 and Lusenda 3
- **Mulongwe:** Block 10, Block 4 and Block 7

#### A tailored installation strategy

One key lesson from Phase 1: the two camps have different structures and needs. In Phase 2, a separate approach was therefore developed for each camp:

| Criterion | Mulongwe | Lusenda |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Installation site | Directly at water points (standpipes, pumps) | Strategic public locations |
| Primary goal | Filtering all water used in the household | Filtering for direct consumption on site |
| Reach | Broad – all households | Targeted – public spaces |

<Figure src="wp/2025/11/wewater-lusenda-mulongwe-dr-kongo-6.jpg" alt="Access to Clean Water for Internally Displaced Persons in Refugee Camps in the D.R. Congo (photo 5)" />

Installing the filters directly at the water points in Mulongwe is a step change in quality: here, not only drinking water but **all water used in the household** is filtered. This considerably reduces the risk of contamination at the source. In Lusenda, by contrast, the focus is on publicly accessible sites where a particularly large number of people can be reached.

In the event of an epidemiological emergency – such as a cholera outbreak – the filters can be flexibly moved to other locations to enable a rapid response.

#### Transporting the filters

The six new filters went through several logistical stages:

1. Import from Germany via the international airport in Bujumbura, Burundi
2. Customs clearance by a licensed customs agency in Burundi and at the Congolese border in Uvira
3. Overland transport from Bujumbura to Uvira and temporary storage at the APM office in Uvira
4. **Transport by water** in motorised pirogues across **Lake Tanganyika** from Uvira to Baraka – the road on this section was impassable due to its poor condition
5. Four-wheel-drive transport from Baraka to the camps

Despite the complex route, all filters reached their destinations undamaged and in line with the action plan agreed with WeWater.

#### Local ownership and continuous monitoring

<Figure src="wp/2025/11/wewater-lusenda-mulongwe-dr-kongo-3.jpg" alt="Access to Clean Water for Internally Displaced Persons in Refugee Camps in the D.R. Congo (photo 6)" />

Before each installation, APM’s technical team – together with the CNR and the WASH committee – held awareness sessions in **Kirundi and Swahili**. They covered how the filters work, how to maintain and use them correctly, and key hygiene rules.

To ensure long-term support, **two local focal persons were formally employed** in Phase 2 to take care of monitoring, maintenance and reporting. Every month they produce reports on the condition of the filters and how often they are used, which the WASH project management compiles on a quarterly basis.

#### Challenges we continue to face

Despite the progress made, coverage remains incomplete. APM’s project report identifies the following open issues:

- **Insufficient coverage:** even 12 filters are not enough for all households
- **Difficult logistics** in remote areas due to poor road conditions
- **A lack of containers** for safely storing filtered water at home
- **No greywater infrastructure** around the filter sites
- **The need for ongoing training** of the committees in efficient water management

These findings feed directly into the planning of the next project phase.
